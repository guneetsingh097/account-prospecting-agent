Why Barometric Pressure Triggers Migraines

The mechanism: When barometric pressure drops or rises rapidly, the pressure differential between sinus cavities and the outside changes faster than the body can equalise. This triggers the trigeminal nerve — the primary pain pathway in migraines — and can dilate blood vessels in the brain.

Falling vs rising: A falling pressure front (storms approaching) is the strongest trigger for most migraine sufferers. Rising pressure after a prolonged low can also cause rebound episodes. The rate of change matters more than the absolute level.

Thresholds used here: HIGH = ≥4 hPa in 3 hours or ≥10 hPa in 24 hours. MEDIUM = ≥2.5 hPa / 3 h or ≥6 hPa / 24 h. Based on peer-reviewed research, though individual thresholds vary — tracking your son's migraine diary against this app will calibrate his personal threshold over time.

Your planning advantage: Pressure changes typically begin 6–24 h before a weather front arrives. That window is your opportunity: early pre-medication, trigger avoidance, and ensuring good sleep the night before are most effective when started early.
